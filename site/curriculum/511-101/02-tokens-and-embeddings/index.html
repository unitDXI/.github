Tokens and Embeddings

Lesson 2 of 12 · 60 min · Updated 30 September 2026

What you’ll learn

  • Explain how text becomes a sequence of tokens
  • Describe what an embedding vector represents
  • Compute cosine similarity between two embeddings

A language model never sees letters or words. It sees numbers. Two steps turn your text into something a model can work with. Tokenization splits text into pieces and gives each piece an integer ID. Embedding turns each ID, or a whole passage, into a list of numbers that captures meaning. This lesson builds both ideas from scratch in plain Python, then adds the embedding pieces to your course notebook.

Why not just use words?#

The obvious approach is to split on spaces and give every word an ID. It breaks down quickly:

  • The vocabulary never ends. New names, typos, code identifiers and words in other languages keep appearing. Any word missing from the vocabulary becomes an “unknown” token and its meaning is lost.
  • Related forms look unrelated. believe, unbelievable and unbelievably would get three unrelated IDs.
  • Punctuation sticks to words. "words." and "words" would be different entries.

Splitting into single characters fixes the vocabulary problem: there are only so many characters. But sequences become very long, and a single character carries almost no meaning.

['Tokenizers', 'split', 'unbelievably', 'long', 'words.']
['u', 'n', 'b', 'e', 'l', 'i', 'e', 'v', 'a', 'b', 'l', 'y']

Modern tokenizers sit in between. They use subword units: common words become a single token, and rare words are built from a few reusable pieces.

Byte-pair encoding, by hand#

The most widely used way to learn subwords is byte-pair encoding (BPE). It was adapted for language models by Sennrich, Haddow and Birch in 2016. The algorithm fits in one sentence:

Start with individual characters, then repeatedly merge the pair of adjacent symbols that appears most often, until the vocabulary is the size you want.

Here it is on a tiny corpus. We add _ to the end of each word so the tokenizer can learn pieces that only occur at word endings, like est_.

merge 1: 'l' + 'o'
merge 2: 'lo' + 'w'
merge 3: 'e' + 'r'
merge 4: 'er' + '_'
merge 5: 'low' + '_'
merge 6: 'e' + 's'
merge 7: 'es' + 't'
merge 8: 'est' + '_'
low_
low er_
low est_
n e w er_
n e w est_
w i d er_
w i d est_

After eight merges, the tokenizer has learned low, er_ and est_ without being told anything about English. They were simply the most frequent patterns. Real tokenizers run the same loop over billions of words until they reach a vocabulary size set in advance. Most modern models use one with tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand entries. Many also start from raw bytes instead of characters, so no input is ever “unknown”.

Encoding new words#

To tokenize new text, apply the learned merges in the order they were learned. Then look each piece up in the vocabulary to get its ID:

lowest -> ['low', 'est_'] -> [15, 7]
newest -> ['n', 'e', 'w', 'est_'] -> [18, 5, 27, 7]
slowest -> ['s', 'low', 'est_'] -> [23, 15, 7]

slowest never appeared in the corpus, yet it encodes cleanly into pieces the tokenizer already knows. That is the whole point of subwords. The list of integers on the right is what the model actually receives.

Tip

Token counts drive cost, speed and context limits, so it helps to have a rough sense of them. For English prose, common tokenizers average roughly three to four characters per token. Code, numbers and many other languages usually take more tokens for the same meaning. Always measure with the tokenizer your model actually uses.

From IDs to vectors#

An ID like 15 is just a label. The number 15 is not “closer” to 14 in meaning. A model’s first layer therefore swaps each ID for a learned vector: row 15 of a big table called the embedding matrix. That table has one row per vocabulary entry and a few hundred to several thousand columns. It starts random. During training it gradually arranges itself so that tokens used in similar ways end up with similar vectors.

The same idea applies to whole passages. An embedding model reads a sentence or document and outputs a single vector that summarises its meaning. Those text embeddings power semantic search, clustering and retrieval (lesson 7). In both cases the rule is the same:

Texts with similar meanings get vectors that point in similar directions.

Note

The individual numbers in a learned embedding do not have human-readable meanings. The toy vectors below use four hand-picked dimensions so you can see what is going on. Real embeddings are learned, and only the relationships between vectors are meaningful.

Measuring similarity#

To compare two vectors we care about direction, not length. Cosine similarity measures the angle between them. Take the dot product and divide by the product of the two lengths:

cosine(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖)

The result runs from −1 (opposite directions) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (same direction).

cat vs kitten 0.995
cat vs dog    0.932
cat vs pizza  0.147

As intended, kitten is almost identical to cat, dog is close, and pizza is far away. Semantic search is this same comparison run against thousands or millions of stored vectors.

Finding nearest neighbours#

Search is just “compare the query with everything and sort”:

[('kitten', 0.242), ('dog', 0.192)]
[('cat', 0.995), ('dog', 0.934), ('pizza', 0.242)]

Comparing against every stored vector is fine for thousands of items. For millions, systems use approximate nearest-neighbour indexes, which you will meet in 511-203.

Check your understanding#

Why do tokenizers use subwords instead of whole words or single characters?

Whole words make the vocabulary unbounded, so unseen words become “unknown”. Single characters make sequences long and each unit nearly meaningless. Subwords keep the vocabulary a fixed size, can still spell any word, and store common words as a single token.

In BPE, why must merges be applied in the order they were learned?

Later merges build on earlier ones. For example, lo + w only exists after l + o has been merged. Applying them in another order produces different, and usually longer, token sequences than the ones the model saw during training.

Two embeddings have a cosine similarity of 0.98. What does that tell you, and what doesn’t it?

The vectors point in almost the same direction, so the model treats the texts as very similar in meaning. It says nothing about the vectors’ lengths. It also doesn’t guarantee the texts are factually equivalent: “the drug is safe” and “the drug is unsafe” can embed very close together.

Key takeaways#

  • Models read token IDs, not text. The tokenizer is part of the model and must match it exactly.
  • BPE learns subwords by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair. Common words become one token and rare words become a few.
  • An embedding maps a token or passage to a vector. Similar meanings point in similar directions.
  • Cosine similarity compares direction and ignores length. Semantic search is “embed, compare, sort”.
